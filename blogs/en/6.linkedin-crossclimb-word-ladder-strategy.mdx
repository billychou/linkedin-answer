Crossclimb is LinkedIn's word ladder puzzle: you transform a start word into an end word by changing exactly one letter at each step, and every rung of the ladder must be a valid word. Crossword-style clues accompany the rungs to keep you oriented. Word ladders are one of the oldest puzzle forms in existence — Lewis Carroll invented them in the 1870s — and the techniques that work today are the ones that have survived a century and a half.

## The rules

1. Start word and end word are the same length.
2. Each step changes exactly **one** letter — no adding, removing, or rearranging.
3. Every intermediate word must be a valid English word.
4. Crossword-style clues describe the rungs and help you confirm each step.

A real example ladder: SAID → LAID → LAND → LANE → CANE → CONE → DONE. Notice how each rung changes a single letter, and how the ladder "pivots" — it holds some letters stable while others rotate.

## Technique 1: Work from both ends

The biggest speed win. Beginners climb forward from the start word and get lost in branching possibilities. Instead:

- From the **start** word, note which letters need to become which letters in the **end** word.
- From the **end** word, work backwards one or two steps.
- Meet in the middle, where the two halves have far fewer cells of distance to bridge.

A ladder of six or seven rungs usually splits cleanly into two three-step halves.

## Technique 2: Change the rarest-constraint letter first

Look at the letters that differ between start and end. If one position must change from S to D, and very few words fit `_AID` patterns, resolve that position first. Positions with many candidate words can wait; positions with few candidates decide the shape of the ladder.

## Technique 3: Use pivot words

A pivot word shares letters with both halves of the journey. In SAID → DONE, words like LAID and LAND act as pivots: they keep most letters stable while unlocking the next change. When stuck, ask: what common word differs from my current rung by exactly one letter and also sits closer to the target's letter profile?

## Technique 4: Let the clues verify, not lead

The crossword clues describe the rung words, not the route. Use them as confirmation: if your candidate rung does not match its clue, the rung is wrong — do not bend the clue to fit. Matching rungs to clues is also a great way to unstuck: a clue like "Opposite of hot" instantly identifies a rung even when you have not found the ladder path to it.

## Technique 5: Common letter-swap families

Some letter families appear constantly in ladders and are worth memorizing:

- Vowel swaps: C**A**NE → C**O**NE
- Endings: LAND → LANE (D→E), CONE → DONE (C→D)
- Common prefixes/heads: SAID → LAID (S→L)

Recognizing a family tells you the one-letter move without searching.

## When you hit a dead end

A dead end means one of your rungs locked in a letter that the rest of the ladder cannot use. Retrace to the last rung where you had a genuine alternative and try the other branch. Because the clues pin down each rung's meaning, retracing is cheap: you always know which rungs are already correct.

## Practice

Crossclimb publishes one ladder per day, and ladders vary between gentle (five rungs, common words) and sharp (long pivots, unusual vocabulary). Reviewing solved ladders is genuinely instructive — each one shows a route through word-space you can reuse.
