LinkedIn Queens looks almost too simple: place one crown in every row, every column, and every colored region — and keep the crowns from touching. But those three constraints plus the no-touch rule create a puzzle where a single correct deduction can unlock the whole board. The good news: almost every daily puzzle can be solved with pure logic. Guessing is rarely necessary.

## The rules, precisely

1. Place exactly one crown (👑) in every **row**.
2. Place exactly one crown in every **column**.
3. Place exactly one crown in every **colored region**.
4. Crowns may never touch each other — horizontally, vertically, **or diagonally**.

Since rows, columns, and regions all carry exactly one crown, the number of regions always equals the grid size. That bookkeeping fact is useful: if you have placed crowns in five regions of a six-region board, the last region's crown is constrained by everything you already placed.

## Technique 1: Start with the smallest regions

A region of one or two cells is a gift. A one-cell region tells you exactly where its crown goes. A two-cell region gives you a binary choice — and the no-touch rule plus row/column constraints usually eliminate one of the two options within a move or two.

## Technique 2: Mark eliminations, not just placements

Every placed crown eliminates:

- its entire row,
- its entire column,
- all eight surrounding cells (the no-touch zone).

When you place a crown, immediately mark those cells as dead in your head (or on paper). Most "hard" puzzles become easy once eliminations are tracked honestly — the next forced cell usually stares back at you.

## Technique 3: Use region shape to force cells

When a region has several cells but all except one are eliminated by rows, columns, or no-touch zones, the remaining cell is forced. Conversely, when a row has only one cell left uneliminated, that cell must hold the row's crown — even if it sits in a region you had not planned to touch yet.

Work in both directions: region → cell, and row/column → cell. The puzzle usually opens up at the intersection of the two.

## Technique 4: The diagonal trap

The diagonal rule is the one players forget most. Two cells that sit corner-to-corner cannot both hold crowns, even if they belong to different rows, columns, and regions. When you are stuck, scan for cells that would diagonally trap a region: sometimes a region's only legal cell is determined purely by avoiding a diagonal collision.

## Technique 5: Count to catch mistakes

If you ever feel stuck on a board that looks nearly full, count: crowns placed versus regions remaining, plus a quick check that no row or column holds two. A miscounted crown early on is the usual reason a board becomes "unsolvable". Undoing one bad placement almost always restores the cascade.

## When logic seems to stall

In rare positions, two cells remain as candidates for one region and the rest of the board cannot distinguish them. Before committing to a guess:

- Re-check every elimination — a missed diagonal is the usual culprit.
- Look at the *other* unfinished region instead. Stalls are often solved from the opposite side of the board.

Queens rewards pattern memory: after twenty or thirty puzzles, forced cells and diagonal traps become automatic. Play the daily puzzle, and when you solve it, glance at the explanation anyway — comparing your deduction order with another valid path is one of the fastest ways to sharpen the skill.
